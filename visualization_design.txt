In this visualization design I start from one clear guiding question, which is how weather conditions affect the reliability of MBTA key bus routes. The data has many dimensions, including time, routes, on time performance, rainfall and snowfall, ridership and geography. If everything is shown on one figure the main message becomes very hard to see. For that reason I split the analysis into several pages that form a story. The order moves from overall trends, to the relationship between weather and demand, then to distribution differences under different weather types, then to the mechanism behind ridership changes, and finally to a spatial view of which parts of the city are more vulnerable.

For the overall trend I use a time series line chart with one line for each key bus route. The horizontal axis is time in months and the vertical axis is the percentage of trips that are on time. I aggregate the original daily data to monthly averages. This reduces noise from day to day variation and makes long term changes and seasonal patterns easier to see. When all routes are drawn together they form a band of lines that stays within a certain range. This band gives readers an immediate feeling for the typical reliability level of the whole system and also lets them see periods where the band shifts upward or downward. Differences between routes appear naturally as some lines sit above or below others, but the color scheme does not overemphasize any single route so that readers focus first on system level stability.

In the part about precipitation and demand I use three scatter plots to show relationships. In each plot every point represents one day. The vertical axis is the on time rate of that day. The horizontal axis is daily rainfall in the first plot, daily snowfall in the second, and ridership in the third. Because all three plots share the same vertical axis, readers can compare how the cloud of points sits for each factor. In the rainfall and snowfall plots the overall level of the points does not drop dramatically as precipitation increases, which suggests that typical rain or snow does not automatically crush reliability. In the snowfall plot many days share the same few snow levels so points align in vertical strips. I keep this visual pattern so that readers understand it comes from the discrete nature of snowfall data. In contrast the ridership plot shows that days with very high ridership tend to have lower on time percentages. This visual comparison highlights that demand pressure is often a stronger constraint than weather itself.

To explore differences between weather types in a more global way I use box plots for clear days, rainy days and snowy days. Each box displays the median and the interquartile range of the on time rate, and the whiskers and individual points reveal extreme low reliability days. With this design readers can quickly see what a typical day looks like under each weather condition. A somewhat surprising pattern becomes clear. The median reliability on snowy days is not lower than on other days and in some cases is slightly higher, while clear and rainy days have more concentrated boxes but a slightly lower center. This result encourages readers to think beyond the simple idea that snow must always be the worst and prepares them to consider changes in ridership and operations.

In the visualization of ridership by route and weather I use grouped bar charts. The horizontal axis lists the key bus routes and the vertical axis shows average daily ridership. For each route there are three bars representing clear, rainy and snowy conditions. This layout allows readers to compare two things at once, how ridership differs between routes under the same weather, and how ridership on a single route changes across weather types. The pattern that emerges is that almost all routes carry fewer passengers on snowy days and that the biggest drops often occur on the busiest routes. Combined with the earlier box plot result, this supports the idea that higher reliability in snow is linked to lower demand and fewer boarding delays rather than improvement in traffic conditions.

Finally I connect the analysis back to real space with a map of route reliability. Using line geometry data for the bus routes I draw each key route along its actual path on the city map and color each segment according to its reliability measure. As a result, readers can find the lines they personally use and see whether those corridors tend to perform well or poorly. When the map is filtered by different weather or summarizing choices it reveals that central city corridors and already congested areas more often show lower reliability. In contrast some peripheral or simpler routes look more stable across conditions. This spatial view grounds the previous numerical analysis in familiar streets and neighborhoods and shows where the network is most fragile.

Overall this visualization design is not just a collection of charts but a structured narrative centered on one analytical question. The time series chart builds intuitive understanding of the system over several years. The scatter plots test how weather and demand relate to performance. The box plots reveal how the distribution of reliability shifts under different weather types. The grouped bar charts explain how drops in ridership under snow can coexist with slightly better on time performance. The map then shows where in the city these patterns matter most. By keeping the meaning of axes consistent and using stable color encodings for weather categories, the design reduces cognitive load so that readers can concentrate on patterns and reasoning. The goal is that after reading through the whole sequence, people not only know how reliable MBTA buses are under different weather conditions, but also understand that the observed reliability comes from the combined effect of demand changes and the spatial structure of the network.
